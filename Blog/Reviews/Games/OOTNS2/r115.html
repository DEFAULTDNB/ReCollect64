ReCollect64 Reviews No. 115

The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time [NS2] ~ Nov 12, 2026

Hey, Listen!

1998's The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time (OOT) is an undeniable classic. Nintendo took the formula of their previous 2D Zelda titles and transformed it into a three-dimensional experience like no other. Our silent sword-wielding protagonist could now explore Hyrule Field, take a dip in Lake Hylia, boulder around Death Mountain and interact with numerous interesting characters whose tales are woven through time.

OOT's stanglehold on the N64 top spot (and the highest-rated video game of all time on Metacritic) is well deserved, so how will a modern reimagining of the title fare against its retro counterpart? Over the course of the last week, I have immersed myself in The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time Remake for the Nintendo Switch 2, and now I wish to share my thoughts on it with you.

For those who are somehow unaware, OOT is a tale of light versus dark. Link is charged with the task of protecting Hyrule against the dark forces of Ganondorf. Your journey begins as a child and escalates, through time travel, into adulthood and back again. Completion of the story relies on cleverly putting things in place for your future self and bringing knowledge back with you to the past in order to build bonds with allies and wrangle favour with strangers.

A Sensory Treat

Having lived and breathed the original at launch (1998), and having somehow skipped the 2011 3DS version entirely, 2026's iteration of The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time for the Nintendo Switch 2 is, for me, an unbelievable paradigm shift in terms of graphics, sound and quality of life updates.

Firing up the game, you immediately get a sense of the sheer attention to detail and love that has been layered into this remake. From the moment the game begins and the brand-new cinamatics play, I was hit by waves of nostalgia and feelings of utter amazement at the impressive new graphic direction and revised storytelling. Granular details such as the materials things are made from are intricately detailed and ultra-realistic in the sense that you can see the stitches in the fabric garments, you can feel the tactile flow within woodgrain, and you can physically see wear, tear and imperfections in glinting metal work.

Each biome has a unique look and feel, with living breathing inhabitants, volumetric lighting, layer upon layer of vegetation, steaming vents, sparkling ice, flowing water that ripples and shines, and most impressively, an interactive foggy layer that tumbles and swirls incredibly realistically. Each area has gravitas and builds a believable world that you want to fully immerse yourself within. Having come from the N64 version, this is an astonishing jump in fidelity and micro-level detail that needs to be seen in motion to be truly believed.

Sounds verberate, and each setting has a distinct rhythm and sound within it. The soundtrack is stunning. It doesn't deviate from the original game's music score; instead it elevates it to a grand, symphonic, orchestrally-backed experience that undulates across every area. The nostalgia is palpable when you enter a new area and absorb the reinvigorated high-definition score. It's really impressive and utterly encapsulating throughout the entirety of the adventure.

This latest iteration of the game features a purposefully intricate, hand-sculpted marionette-style character redesign, and it is sewn together with brand-new cutscenes that better explain situations and expand upon the game's lore within the core story. There are also fully-voiced characters (sans Link) that lend more character to each cinematic, and new lines of text that serve to modernise the game a littel and give it yet more depth and character.

(Locations are instantly recognisable even in full 3D. Water caustics and diffraction effects look absolutely incredible in motion)

QOL Updates & Mechanics

I'll rip the Band-Aid off quickly. The iconic GUI is largely gone. The three C-buttons of the original game's HUD are notably absent, but with good reason; the NS2 controller obviously doesn't have the same layout, so it's been modernised. Regardless, I can't help but feel that some of the character is missing on that front, because it was so distintive to have the C-buttons and their customisable functions onscreen.

There is also a jump button. Prior to this version of OOT, Link was fully grounded and only auto-jumped when the situation demanded it. Now you're free to jump around the scenery with the X button, testing out the boundaries and seeing if there are new ways to explore the familiar settings. Though it takes a while to adjust to the new ability, it works, and it works well. Similarly, using the second stick as a free-moving camera also works wonders compared to the N64's system of centring the camera with the Z-trigger, or tapping C-up to view the world from Link's eyes. The latter, by the way, has been removed; you cannot enter first-person view at all in this remake.

The Z-targetting remains largely intact for combat, as does the swordplay, but most notably, the menu system has been entirely reinvigorated. Gone is the cube-like trasitional menu, now there is a compartmental layout that divides items and amenities into child, adult, and ones that can be utilised by both. The menu still has the original sound effects when scrolling through pages. Outside of the menu, you can tap L or R to cycle items for quick use on the ZR button, but again, there is only one button instead of the three C-buttons we had on the N64. Your map is now on the Minus button, and it contains trackers for heart pieces and Gold Skulltulas.

Other new features include a rather neat quest log; Castle Town is now fully 3D and explorable; there is a songbook that displays learned tunes; puzzles and dungeons are slightly rearranged in places; there is a 5th bottle to collect; there are business scrub seeds that can be used on-demand, the lens of truth is now a pair of glasses rather than a sleuth-like magnifying glass; and there is a new mode where you can view all encountered enemies as high-definition models within the museum-like Hall of Fallen Echoes along with options to replay boss and miniboss fights to record better completion times.

(Detailing across everything is incredible. Gohma has plants growing on its armor, and the fishing lake is beautifully tranquil)

Outstanding Gameplay

Exploration of Hyrule's entirety and the interaction of the NPCs within it is the number one aim of this game, as it always was. You learn information, discover issues that need solving, and forge allies by helping them in their times of need. As you progress, the game allows you to choose your own path. You don't need to go to Lon Lon Ranch or Kakariko Village first; you could deviate straight to Lake Hylia, or take a stroll around Castle Town, taking in the sights and finding secrets and side quests. Bear in mind that some tasks require you to be a child when the world is sweetness and light, and others when you jump forward in time seven years and the world is in a dark, decrepit and dangerous state. Equally some things only happen at night, so pay attention to the day night cycle, or just play the Sun's song if you've got it.

With three dungeons to tame as a child and a further six plus the final Castle as an adult, there are plenty of puzzles, weapons, items, upgrades, heart pieces and spells to obtain. The thirst for more is real, and I found myself constantly wanting to find the next item to unlock additional things in each area. For example, you can shoot distant Gold Skulltulas, but you can't collect their golden skull token without the boomerang, so there is a constant draw to come back and re-look at each area once you have a fresh piece of equipment to hand or a new tune to play on your trusty ocarina.

Everything is as I remembered it but better. Since I played this game last in full (which was probably pre Y2K), I hadn't realised just how much I had forgotten too. I even confused parts with The Legend of Zelda: Majoras Mask, a game I have definitely re-played more than this title. Ultimately, replaying this was like getting a warm hug from an old friend. It feels like you've never been apart. Pure nostalgia flows through every fibre, and the feeling of amazement and the sensation of adventure still easily holds up today.

I thoroughly enjoyed the exploration and adventure elements of the game, and more so, the dungeons. Navigating Hyrule Field in 2026, it feels a little smaller, but then I'm a lot bigger now. Proceeding games in the series such as Breath of the Wild and Tears of the Kingdom have also expanded my horizons concerning scale and scope.

Regardless of Hyrule Field feeling a little sparse, solving the dungeons is where my heart truly lies within this game. Each is unique and offers item-specific puzzles and intricate bosses that make the game so remarkably playable. Every boss has been artistically redrawn with astoundingly high definition detail whilst retaining the original silhouette and character. There are little nuances added here and there to amp-up their individuality, but the level of detail across the board is truly magnificent.

(Hyrule looks gorgeous throughout the day-night cycle. Boss fights and cinematics have been reworked to look even more impressive)

Spiritual Success

If you had hopes that this remake be the game rebuilt with more content added, then you might be a tad disappointed, but for die-hard OOT demographic who know what they want in a remake, this will not fail to please. I had hoped to see a little beta content to be restored, or perhaps Master Quest to be an option from day one, but I should have known that, Nintendo being Nintendo, the code for Master Quest does appear to be within the game's files (thanks to the work of fastidious data miners), but it will probably arrive as an update or as part of the NSO + Expansion Pak.

Overall I feel like this is yet another stunning Nintendo 64 game that has been remade for a modern audience. It's a graphical showcase which sets the bar for remakes and delivers stunning gameplay and the beloved enjoyable core narrative. Apart from the modern QOL changes and the odd change from a lens to the granny-like spectacles of truth, the game is largely intact and ushers in nostalgia through a renewed sense of discovery and exploration. Notably, it also hasn't been overtly censored or modified to pander towards any particular crowds, though obviously Ganondorf's red blood and certain religious chants within the backing track remain long-gone since the 1.2 revision of the Nintendo 64 cartridge.

If I had one complaint, it would be that the new voice acting is a little over the top in places. Where the majority of voices are nicely portrayed, the breathy anime-like tones of others become grating, and the Great Deku Tree's voice didn't quite sit well with me either.

I just have a few heart pieces and a couple of gold Skulltulas left to get, but I can't wait to keep playing to achieve 100%

The Pros:

 Incredible effects, soundtrack, SFX and art direction.
 Modern controls are refreshing.
 20-35 hours of content for veterans or completionists.
 QOL updates make it even more playable.
 Zelda notes app compatible for in-game assistance.
 Cutscenes add to the experience.
 Hall of Echoes is a great addition.
The Cons:

 Only one save file and no name input.
 Some voice acting was grating.
 Master Quest is NOT included.
Conclusion:

The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time demonstrates just how expertly a 28-year-old game can be reinvigorated through a swish graphical overhaul and authentically retaining the core characters and themes so that veteran players and a new generation can experience freshly.

You can see how this game sold 5.2 million copies in just 4 days; it's a console selling game that you should not ignore if you own a Nintendo Switch 2, and if you don't, now is the time to buy one!
