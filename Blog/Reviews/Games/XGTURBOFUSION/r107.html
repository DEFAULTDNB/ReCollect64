Extreme G Turbo Fusion ~ Aug 3, 2026

Extreme Examination

Extreme-G: Turbo Fusion is the first double-pack/multi-game cartridge by ModRetro, which is licensed by Throwback Entertainment. The cartridge contains both Extreme-G and Extreme-G 2 on one cartridge and has a simple game-select menu at the start whereby you select the logo of the game you wish to play. For those who were unaware, Throwback Entertainment has owned IP rights to Extreme-G and Extreme G 2 since 2006, when Acclaim sadly had to file for bankruptcy and sold off all of their properties and trademarks.

Fast-Paced Futurism

When I first heard that Extreme-G was coming back in 2026, my mind started racing. The game that blew me away with its blisteringly fast speeds in 1997 would be gracing my livingroom in an all-new form as a re-release via ModRetro. But my biggest question was, "Are they simply a re-release, or are they going to improve them somehow?"

When Acclaim handed over the rights to these phenomenally fast games back in 2006, I had no doubt that Throwback Entertainment would now be sole owners of not only the ROMs but the source code as well. I wondered if fixes, patches, QOL updates, or even new content would arise from this modern version. Alas, I don't think that anything has been done to these games. They appear to play exactly the same as before, albeit with modified introductory screens that tell everyone this is now Throwback's IP, rather than Acclaim and Probe Entertainment.

The two games, which total 20 MB worth of ROMs, are packed into a rather nice translucent yellow cartridge, a brand new shade for the system, which originally had only 6 solid colours. Internally, much like the Xibalba 64 cartridge, this is powered by a GW1N-LV9EQ144C6/I5 FPGA; however, unlike Xibalba 64, which works on any region, the release of Extreme-G: Turbo Fusion is limited to running on an NTSC system due to using the NTSC-U builds of the games on the cartridge.

As far as I can tell, both games are identical to their original releases. There doesn't appear to be any additional content added, no improvements made to optimise or clean up the game, and crucially, nothing has been removed either. All the music, names and tracks are intact and ready to blast around at face-melting speeds, with spupport for the rumble pak and memory pak retained too.

The Speed We Need

Beginning with Extreme-G, the game feels identical to how I remember it. There is a selection of 12 tracks, 8 vehicles plus 2 additional bikes to unlock, and a total of 8 modes across single and multiplayer, which allows for up to four players to compete head-to-head.

Visually, I found Extreme-G to be quite vibrant but simplistic. Lighting effects are used to draw your eye around each track, giving you a little foresight into which direction you need to steer towards next. I like the visual juxtaposition of rocky, natural terrain punctuated by strobing RGB lighting and the striking graphic queues of the various collectable power-ups.

When the game gets up to speed and the music is pumping, it's incredibly difficult to control, at least initially. There is a steep learning curve to these games that require you to know the tracks and know how to ricochet or skim off walls when careening into them. After half an hour or so I was 90% better at steering than when I'd first picked up the controller, and it felt great to have some palpably positive progression.

Extreme-G 2 expands on the original somewhat, expanding the roster to 13 bikes and 12 tracks with 3 variations on each one, plus mirror races, but there are just 7 main modes within the game, effectively removing the multiplayer flag collecting option from the first game.

Building on the first game's core vision, the sequel ramped everything up with more environmental variation and effects such as lightning. To race, the vehicles feel heavier, with each one having more distinct attributes for turning, acceleration and defence/firepower. The original game's vehicles felt less individual and more like jazzy skins over one type of bike.

At maximum velocity, XG2 becomes visually choppy and tough to handle without oversteering. The sequel is definitely pushing the hardware more than the first, but it becomes somewhat of a slideshow when multiple vehicles and effects are on-screen at once. The music in XG2, in my humble opinion, is far superior to that of the first game. It's moodier, more bass-heavy and more atmospheric, pulling away from a WipeOut 64 dance music vibe and carving out its own big beat/drum-and-bass-laden itentity. I like it a lot!

Multiplayer and More

There is nothing quite like plugging four controllers into a console and digging into an evening of split-screen multiplayer. Extreme-G: Turbo Fusion has this in spades thanks to the twin titles' combined multitude of tracks and multiplayer modes. The Extreme-G games never quite exceeded Mario Kart 64 or Diddy Kong Racing's appeal as multiplayer racers back in the day, but they were always pulled out towards the end of a session to see who could handle the rapid races.

This time around, I think we enjoyed Extreme-G's racing more than Extreme-G 2's, but the battle modes of the sequel were more enjoyable than those of the original. These tank battles add an extra layer of carnage and tactics to each game's multiplayer modes; they're a strange but decent addition that adds arena-based variety against the otherwise strictly linear races.

If you really want to get strange, use the "Unlock everything" code on XG2 (pictured above), and then enter your name as "2064" to unlock the WipeOut-style paper plane-looking vehicles. With this unlocked you have access to every racer, every track, mirror races, and even an on-foot mode where your characters run at breakneck speed without the need for a vehicle!

The Pros:

 Two speedy packages in one.
 Lovely transparent yellow cartridge.
 Plenty of multiplayer modes between them.
 The soundtrack is still fierce and moody.
 All original cheat codes still work (NA version).
 Runs perfectly on OG N64 & FPGA devices.
The Cons:

 No new content or QOL updates.
 No clever way to swap games (need to press reset).
Conclusion:

Extreme-G: Turbo Fusion is an odd ball. On one hand it's great to see games getting a re-release, but on the other, you could buy each individual game on cartridge on the secondhand market (£5.95 + £9.95 at the time of writing), which is less than half the cost of this £46 double-pack.

I would have liked to have seen a QOL update to the framerates, or textures, or at least an additional track or exclusive vehicle added, just to flex over the original releases, but this is quite simply a multicart re-release of two classic racing games.

Which games do we think will get the next multi-cart treatment? Perhaps the Banjo-Kazooie duology? Maybe even the Mortal Kombat, Tony Hawk or Turok trilogies? What about the NFL Blitz quadrology? Who knows!
