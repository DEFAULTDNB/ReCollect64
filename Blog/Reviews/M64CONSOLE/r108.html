ReCollect64 Reviews No. 108

ModRetro M64 Console ~ Aug 6, 2025
ModRetro M64 Console

Unboxing My Feelings

'FPGA' is quite the buzzword these days. The M64 uses a 16nm AMD Artix UltraScale FPGA and a modified Nintendo 64 core, which was originally developed by Robert Piep, to emulate the system at a hardware level. Field Programmable Gate Arrays, or FPGAs, are essentially versatile microchips that can duplicate electronic circuits and logic gates, mimicking a system at a hardware level. In this case, the RCP (RDP and RSP) and the CPU of the Nintendo 64.

ModRetro are the latest to get into this area of hardware emulation, but since the Analogue 3D was released in November of last year, it now has some stiff competition when it comes to dominating the Nintendo 64-clone-console space, especially with the A3D's latest updates, which have almost entirely fleshed out the system to be far better than it was at release. I am going to examine the M64 for what it is, how it compares, and what it needs to compete.

Unboxing the console is a weird experience, especially as a collector. Every edge and every surface of the outrageously pink and yellow outer cardboard box is embellished with something snazzy, bright and punchy, yet to open the box you have to physically rip a strip off, making the box unable to ever be closed tightly ever again. The inner box is black and finished with a glossy illustration of the outline of the console which is nice, but the decision to make the outer packaging a one-time experience is baffling.

(I opted for the gorgeous AMD Red variant and the matching pro controller)

Guided Tour

Moving around the console, you have an LED ring around each of the four controller sockets, which are vibrant pink and pleasing to behold when fully populated. On the rear you have your I/O, which consists of a USB-C power input socket, two USB-C acessory ports, and a spring-loaded MicroSD card for storing firmware updates and settings. I much prefer the MicroSD as well as the click-in/click-out eject method to the Analogue 3D's 3/4 sized OG SD card that needs yanking out with no mechanism.

On the top you have your power button on the left, the menu-controlling dial on the right, the centralised cart slot with a "light pipe", and an eject button towards the top-middle. The eject button is actually quite nice to have; it does feel like it's prolonging the life of the console by minimising stress on the cart slot. It is as powerful as people say too. If you don't hold your cart as you press it, you can launch your game 2-3 feet into the air quite easily.

A particularly odd feature of the console is that aforementioned light pipe. Inspired by how museums light up oil paintings, this little LED bar lights up your cart art. It's a nice touch if a little unnecessary, but it does double as a progress bar and indicator of issues, should you update the console or run into any troublesome cartridges that need a clean.

ModRetro appears to take everything to extremes, from the box art to the eject button and all the minor details besides. The build quality is also equally as extreme. This thing is rock-solid and built to last. I have to admit that in the AMD red colourway, it looks gorgeous, especially with the transparencies showing off the circuitboard and components within. I feel that I made a great choice with this variant, though the majority of you thought purple was the way to go, winning my recent poll by 1% over the red one. I wasn't so sure and stuck with my gut.

Operating The System

Turning on the console, you are essentially greeted with a screen that asks you to connect a controller and insert a cartridge. Crucially, if you don't have a controller to hand, you can use the menu dial on the right-hand side of the console to control the process of navigating the OS menus. The wheel is a strange device that kind of feels bolted on just because they could. I suppose it's useful in a bind, but what kind of bind are you in where you don't have a controller to hand when operating an N64-based console?

The OS itself is hidden until you either boot up without a cartridge inserted, press the wheel, or press "Start" + "Z" on any compatible controller. The OSD then takes over inputs, and you get a jazzy menu that mirrors the yellow and magenta aesthetic of the outer packaging and has a vibrant and bold tone that's clear to read, even at a distance. The menu contains options for display adjustment, controller pairing, general settings, experimental settings, and console information & updating.

There were two resolution options 4K (2160p) or 1080p, image size (integer+) and fit (stretch) modes as well as four options for display profile, including clean, scanlines, CRT and custom. The latter brings up options for adding additional scanlines, or increasing sharpness and adjusting brightness via grille, horizontal bloom and gamma. I have to admit that the level of control over the screen settings is fantastic, because you can adjust it exactly to your personal preference.

The controller connection screen is self-explanitory, hold down the pairing button (Start) on the controller, and hit pair, or plug one in. The WiFi connection settings are equally as familiar, adopting an on-screen keyboard to input your SSID. I did notice that the console doesn't detect 5 GHz bands, though I don't suppose it needs superfast connectivity just for updating. Having said that, my initial update to version 1.23, which is a 7 MB file, took just shy of 10 minutes to download and install, which seemed excessive. Updating the controller is done on PC with a WebUSB-enabled browser such as Chrome. I will go into far more detail with the controller in its very own review in the coming days.

Speaking of controllers, the 8BitDo 64, Hyperkin and RetroFighters controllers all also work with the M64, but some Bluetooth pads, like the NSO N64 controller for example, will require a BT adaptor, such as the BlueRetro or RetroTime dongles. Wired controllers all appear to work fine too, with me testing a wide variety of pads, wheels and sticks that have been reviewed on the site previously.

(The console and controller match beautifully, and each are rock solid in terms of build-quality)

Core Combobulation

Once you slot in a game and get going, the core experience is great. The console performs well overall, albeit with decidedly muddier graphics than I was expecting. At £211 (£188.09 with the early bird code) for the console, it mirrors the original's $229.99 price point in the USA; however, it doesn't have a pad, and doesn't come with an HDMI cable, whereas the OG console at least came with an RF module or an AV cable for display connectivity.

The lack of a supplied HDMI cable and a basic controller is one thing, but something else stuck out to me; there is no on-board save mechanism. To be fair, the OG console didn't come with a memory card either, and the A3D did the same thing but eventually Analogue added save states to mitigate a lack of saving. I feel that the M64 should have prioritised saving to be a day-one feature. As a result, if a person were to buy an M64 console, a Pro Controller and a copy of Extreme-G: Turbo Fusion, how would they save their progress (Don't say passwords)? It would be a frustrating experience to newcomers to the system who want to join in, or those unaware of the N64's nuances.

Personally, I had hoped that the mistakes made by Analogue might shape the way ModRetro would drive their hardware, but it appears that they are making their own, very comparable mistakes and bringing essential features to the M64 with a similar "better late than never" mentality that Analogue has. It took Analogue 8 months to release all the features they promised, except for WiFi updates. On the flipside, ModRetro doesn't have any of those features yet but does have WiFi updating, albeit painfully slowly. In retrospect, perhaps I was too harsh on Analogue for not including the OTA update features. Thanks to the M64, I'm not convinced we absolutely need it anymore, even for convenience.

I'm sure I remember ModRetro ensuring influencers and testers that on-board saves would launch with the console; however, for some reason or another, that hasn't happened. The solution to the memory pak save problem (cart saves work perfectly fine) for now, is one of three options: firstly, a BlueRetro dongle with built-in memory banks; secondly, a wireless controller with built-in memory (8BitDo 64); or lastly, a wired OG controller with an actual memory pak plugged in (preferably FRAM).

(The OSD menu is certainly eye-catching, but perhaps not the optimal layout. A quick benchmark of the pro controller; a full review will follow soon)

Cooperative Carts

Testing the SummerCart64 and ED64 X7, they worked flawlessly once updated to the latest OS and bootloader. Homebrew games like Driving Strikers 64, 3D Pinball 64, Relictum 64 and all of the previous N64Brew Game Jam ROMs, all of which are made with Libdragon, performed well overall, with no overclocking and scanlines enabled to blur any odd textures in motion. The one thing I did notice was that Sodium 64 converted SNES ROMs that work fine on the OG N64 and the A3D do not work on the M64. Graphically they're glitchy and unplayable, which is quite disappointing.

In terms of ROM hacks, the big ones such as Mario Kart Amped Up, Zelda 64: Dawn & Dusk, GoldenEye X, and the 30 FPS modded Diddy Kong Racing and the English-translated Wave Race 64: Shindou Edition V1.2 worked perfectly, but sadly Kaze Emanuar's Return to Yoshi Island demo 2 did not want to run at all. I tried loading SM64 prior to it to ensure it booted, which was a thing you needed to do back when it was released, but had no joy. Clone carts, or repros, also worked fine. I could only test a repro of Conker's Bad Fur Day and Mission: Impossible, but both worked without a hitch.

Real game carts all worked as expected barring a couple. For reference, there are 16 games that are stated as exhibiting issues on the official M64 compatibility page, and of those, I can confirm that Armorines, Donkey Kong 64, Perfect Dark and Turok 2 all do have slow FPS or frame dips in places. The net result isn't that these ones aren't unplayable, just that they have annoying slowdowns, which is not indicative of 1:1 mimicry of the original hardware. On that list of 16 with issues, 6 contain random crashes or visual issues and should therefore, in my opinion, be considered to be currently unplayable. These seven include Chameleon Twist, Hydro Thunder, Jet Force Gemini, Rush 2049, Rogue Squadron, and StarCraft 64.

Seeing as the beta firmwares contained a heck of a lot more sketchy titles than the 16 left on that list, it's encouraging to hope that soon all 293 games on their list will be 100% functional.

The "Experimental features" listed as coming soon in the menu are extensive, but it is a shame to see things like virtual control paks, progressive output and low-latency video output omitted for the initial release. Overclocking is included but is not very stable in my opinion. A few times games black-screened on me when put into any mode other than off, and one of which was Extreme-G: Turbo Fusion, the one game I really wanted to see fly!

Tips & Tricks

For best results when starting out with your M64, use an HDMI 2.0+ cable (you don't get any HDMI cable supplied), do not use it through an HDMI splitter, and update via SD card (M64.BIN on the root of the SD card) instead of over WiFi for the quickest way to jump into the action with the latest fixes and features. Update your controller via the web updater (a browser with WebUSB compatibility is required) as frequently as possible to get the best results, settings and features. When doing so, turn the pad on first, then connect the Pro Controller to your PC using a data-capable cable, not the USB-C to USB-C power cable supplied with the controller. Not giving us an adequate cable is yet another weird oversight.

You can remotely reset the console by pressing "Start" + "Z", navigating to the system tab and tapping up 3 times to get to "Reboot Core". The function of turning off and resetting the console should be more easily accessible, in my opinion, like on the first tab when you access the OSD menu, because it's all well and good that you can hold the start button for 3 seconds to boot up the console with a paired controller, but you cannot easily turn the console off or reboot it from a distance without scrolling through a bunch of menus and options.

Single tapping the power button turns it off, but double tapping the power button performs a soft reset for those who still like to use the console's physical buttons. Using Integer+ with the clear display mode & customised sharpening is best for those who like it bright and anti-aliased. Scanlines and no sharpening offer more authentic visuals, especially when paired with grille dimming.

The Pros:

 $20-$40 cheaper than the Analogue 3D.
 Easy to set up and play.
 Decent compatibility for carts & accessories.
 Nicle customisable display modes.
 16gb ejectable MicroSD card.
 Moddable, reshellable design.
 AV connector and Chromatic TF compatible.
 Light pipe and jog wheel add extra pizazz.
The Cons:

 No HDMI cable included.
 No virtual memory pak.
 No save/load states.
 No screenshot function.
 No 100% compatibility.
 Fuzzier visual quality.
 Overclocking was spotty.
 OTA update is painfully slow.
Conclusion:

The M64 is a brash, retro-inspired, colourful, characterful, LED-accented console which juxtaposes that of its competitors' brutal and modern minimalism. If the Analogue 3D had not just masterfully timed the release of their 1.5.0 update to coincide with the M64's launch, which brings horizontal upscaling, the M64's graphical ability would have looked visually closer to its closest FPGA neighbour's. As a result, the M64 is plainly second best in terms of graphic fidelity at this moment in time with fuzzier, less-stable graphical output. However, as a piece of hardware, it oozes pizazz and possibility.

The OS on the A3D is markedly better too, because cart art for your played collections is a nice visual touch, as well as jointly storing stats for played time, screenshots and memories neatly within its menu. The M64 has vibrance and personality in spades but no killer features (yet), not as many awesome display modes (BVM/PVM/Progressive/32-bit colour depth), doesn't have 100% compatibility yet (which is a massive downer), and hasn't delivered anything to more easily facilitate saving games for all.

Overall the M64 has a lot of catching up to do. When the A3D released, it too suffered teething issues, and I initially gave it a 7/10 upon release with similar criticism. I will do a mini re-review on both consoles when they each reach one year of age to see where we are at with relative parity or platform-exclusive attributes. As an open-source device, will the ModRetro receive improved/additional/alternative cores? We will just have to wait and see.
